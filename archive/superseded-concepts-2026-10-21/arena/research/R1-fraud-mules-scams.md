Researched 2026-10-05. Doctrine applied: extract, do not infer. Anything I could not find is marked NOT FOUND. All arithmetic was run in Python (script output summarised in "Computed checks").

## How to read the quote column

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| [L] | Literal text I parsed locally from a PDF (FIC report, two arXiv papers). Highest confidence. |
| [F] | Text returned by the WebFetch tool. That tool passes pages through a small summarising model, so treat quotation marks as "as extracted" and re-verify against the live page before it goes on a slide. |
| [S] | Search-result snippet only. I did not open the page (blocked or not fetched). Lowest confidence; treat as a lead. |
| (computed) | My own Python arithmetic on quoted numbers. Not a source claim. |

Dates: where a page gave no date I say so. Several 2026 news dates come from page metadata and could be off by a few days.

## Gaps and surprises

### What would make a product idea stronger

1. A government primary source names the youth-mule link. The FIC says mules are "often unemployed, pensioners, and young South Africans such as students" and are recruited through false "transaction manager" adverts. GroundUp adds that accounts are openly bought on Facebook Marketplace and "They target students and unemployed people." This is the cleanest evidence for a "protect the would-be mule" feature.
2. Mules are often existing, long-standing, legitimate customers (anonymous banker, GroundUp). The person holding the account is the point of intervention, and the bank already has that person's data and attention in the app. No SA bank programme aimed at this person was found.
3. Banking app = 88.6% of 2025 digital crime cases (70.5% of value), yet SABRIC says the apps were not compromised. The fraud is coached, authorised activity inside the app session. That makes in-app friction and warnings the control point, which an FNB App competition favours.
4. SAFPS describes mule accounts "opened weeks in advance and emptied within hours", a concrete lifecycle signal.
5. Scam-baiting AI has quantified precedent (Cybera 17.66% unconditional and 31.74% conditional mule-account disclosure; Apate with CBA) and a mule-extraction use case. No SA bank scam-baiting programme was found, and no source documents SA languages in these systems: white space for a localised version.
6. CBA open-sourced its transaction-description abuse model (AITD via H2O.ai). It reduces build cost for any "abuse in payment references" idea, and CBA's own roadmap lists "foreign language coverage" as unfinished.
7. Pain is concentrated in the right segment: youth unemployment 47.4% (15-34), 5.0m unemployed youth, official rate 33.6% (Q2 2026).

### What would weaken or kill it

1. No count of SA mule accounts, no FNB mule or fraud figures, no national count of fake-job or fake-accommodation victims. Any sizing must be built from proxies and labelled as an estimate (doctrine 7). Everything a prototype produces must carry the `sim_` prefix.
2. The "mule epidemic" story is mixed. SAFPS reports money-mule listings fell 33% in 2024 (while account misuse rose 24%). The NFO figure is only about 666 Ombud cases (computed) and its own text says "suspected fraud". The 2023 SAFPS demographic is 78.5% aged 20-39, not students.
3. FNB reported "a slight reduction in the number of reported scams in FY2024". A pitch that implies FNB is losing the fraud fight conflicts with its own statements.
4. Scam-baiting protects the bank only if intelligence is operationalised. Apate's own numbers imply about 1.2 minutes of scammer time per call on average (computed), so most "waste" is volume. Daisy's value was media reach (a marketing case study), not documented fraud reduction. The only extraction evidence comes from Cybera, a vendor, on English email scammers, with a human reviewing every message. Cybera and Apate are funded competitors ($8.7M and $2.5M seed).
5. Data-sharing friction in SA: a banker says POPIA "slows down information sharing between banks". Mule intelligence is most useful if SAFPS/SABRIC can consume it.
6. Legal: only RICA s4 participant monitoring was found (snippet-level). POPIA, entrapment and regulator sign-off for a bank-run honeypot are not researched. Do not claim legality.
7. Voice cloning is weakly evidenced in SA: SABRIC says "isolated cases"; the quantified items are vendor identity-verification statistics (Smile ID) or small-base percentage spikes. Do not lead with it.
8. The SABRIC 2024 headline numbers conflict with the 2025 report's restated 2024 series (64,000 vs 97,547 incidents; R1.4bn vs R1.86-1.9bn). Cite only the 2025-report series. I never read the SABRIC PDF itself; verify against it before quoting.
9. Language claim cannot be grounded: no SA-language scam dataset found on Hugging Face (Hub search) and none confirmed on Kaggle; the one "Africa smishing" set is synthetic English. A multilingual feature needs synthetic or hand-built data and must be labelled `sim_`. No source says what language SA scam messages use, and FNB app language coverage is NOT FOUND (only an undated call-centre language note).

### Unexpected items worth a second look

- NSFAS allowances are paid into student bank accounts (snippet only), and a student-targeted SRC-member scam involved those allowances (R14,000). Students holding new accounts with a regular deposit may be an attractive mule pool; this is my inference, not a sourced claim.
- Students in the UJ/NSFAS case were paid R2.2m to sign bogus leases (141 students, TimesLive 2026-08-24): a mule-like "lend your identity for cash" pattern inside the education system.
- Australia's ANZ found students and unemployed together at one-third of scam-fund recipients; no SA bank publishes the equivalent. If FNB holds this data internally, it is the single most valuable number the team could ask for.
- Standard Bank, GoTyme and the NFO all published mule warnings in April to July 2026: competitors are already messaging this. FNB's last mule-specific statement found is from July 2023.
